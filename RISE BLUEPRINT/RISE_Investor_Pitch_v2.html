The feature nobody else has built

What if missing your alarm
cost you actual money?

Here is the feature that makes RISE genuinely unlike anything else on the market. When setting an alarm, a user can choose to put real money on the line.

How it works

Emeka sets his alarm for 6:00am. Before confirming, he chooses: "Add a stake."

He enters ₦2,000. He links it to his account via BVN — Nigeria's Bank Verification Number, the identity layer that connects every Nigerian bank account. He chooses what happens if he does not wake up: the money goes to a charity of his choice.

Or — if he has a friend who also uses RISE — it goes directly to them. A friendly bet between two people who both want to wake up early.

The alarm fires. He completes the wake game. His money stays in his wallet. He did it.

Tomorrow, he will not snooze. Not because of willpower. Because ₦2,000 is on the line and it is his.

This is behavioural economics applied to the morning. Research from Dan Ariely, Richard Thaler, and the broader loss-aversion literature is unambiguous: people work twice as hard to avoid losing money they already have than to gain the same amount. A ₦2,000 loss feels worse than a ₦2,000 gain feels good. RISE weaponises that asymmetry for the user's benefit.

🏦

The BVN integration is what makes this real for Nigeria specifically. BVN (Bank Verification Number) is a biometric-linked identity system that connects to every Nigerian bank account. It means the stake is genuinely connected to real money — not a fake in-app token. For Kenya, M-Pesa plays the same role. For South Africa, it is linked bank accounts. This feature works precisely because African mobile banking infrastructure is now sophisticated enough to support it. Western app developers have not noticed this yet.

The charity options are curated and trusted — verified organisations working in health, education, and food security across the user's country. The user chooses their cause during setup. When they miss an alarm with a stake on it, the donation is automatic, instant, and receipted. They get a notification: "Your ₦2,000 went to [charity]. Don't let it happen again."

This feature does not need to be used by every RISE user to be enormously valuable. It needs to be used by the committed ones — the people who are serious enough about changing their mornings to put skin in the game. Those users are exactly the ones most likely to subscribe, refer friends, and stay forever.

The sleep frequency — and why it matters

This is the part that sounds unusual until you understand the science. RISE plays a specific low-frequency audio tone as you settle to sleep — in the delta range (0.5–4 Hz), delivered as binaural beats. Your brain, through a process called entrainment, gradually synchronises its electrical activity with the frequency it is hearing. Delta brainwaves are the brainwave state of deep sleep. The audio guides you there faster.

More importantly for waking up: RISE begins shifting the frequency upward 20–30 minutes before your alarm fires. From delta, toward theta (4–8 Hz, light sleep), then toward alpha (8–12 Hz, relaxed wakefulness). By the time the alarm sounds, your brain is already on its way up. You are not being yanked from the bottom of a sleep cycle. You are meeting the alarm on your way to the surface.

🎵

This is not sleep music. Sleep music helps people relax. What RISE plays is a precision tool for sleep state management — going down at bedtime, coming up before the alarm. Calm sells comfort. RISE sells a smarter morning, engineered from the night before. These are entirely different products.

The result is that RISE users who use the full system — bedtime alarm, sleep frequency, wake-up frequency ramp, then the alarm — report waking up feeling qualitatively different. Not just awake, but ready. That is a meaningful and shareable product experience. It is the thing people tell their friends about.
